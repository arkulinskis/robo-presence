From parts bin to driving in an afternoon

Pick the parts

Wheels, a pan-tilt head, lights, a horn, a sonar… Choose them in the Parts tab: it draws the wiring and writes the Arduino sketch for you.

Install with one line

Flash Raspberry Pi OS, then paste one curl … | sudo bash on the Pi. It installs the robot code, pairs the robot with your account and starts it.

Drive

Press Drive. Video and controls go straight between your browser and the robot, typically in 50 to 200 milliseconds.

Your robot tells the cockpit what it has

When it starts, the robot sends a short list of its parts. The cockpit turns each one into the right control. No hard-coded buttons, so tomorrow's parts just work.

Everything a robot pilot needs

Live video, peer to peer

WebRTC carries the camera to you and your commands to the robot directly, like a video call. The server only makes the introduction.

A real cockpit

Joystick, WASD, a gamepad, drag the picture to look, three gears, an emergency stop, snapshots and clip recording.

It adapts to your robot

The robot reports the parts it has and the cockpit draws switches, sliders, hold buttons, a voice box and gauges to match. Add a part, it shows up.

Pi, Arduino or an old phone

Wire everything to a Pi, let an Arduino run the motors and report its parts, or put a phone on the robot and run it from a browser tab.

Share the keys

Invite pilots and viewers by email, or send a guest-pass link that runs out. Grandparents don't need an account.

Safe by default

The wheels stop by themselves half a second after the signal drops, when you leave, and on STOP. Admins never get your camera.

Questions

What do I need to build one?

A Raspberry Pi 4 or 5 (a Zero 2 W works at low resolution), a Pi camera or USB webcam, a chassis with two motors (or tank tracks), a motor driver such as the TB6612FNG, two hobby servos on a pan-tilt bracket, and a battery with a 5 V regulator. An Arduino Nano, a sonar, LEDs and a buzzer are optional extras. The build guide has the full list and the wiring.

Why WebRTC, and how fast is it?

Most web apps send everything through a server, which adds delay. Robo Presence uses WebRTC, the technology behind browser video calls: your browser and the robot connect directly, so video and controls skip the server. On home Wi-Fi expect around 50 to 150 ms; across the internet, 100 to 300 ms. The cockpit shows the round trip live.

Does it work when the robot is somewhere else?

Usually, yes: the robot only needs Wi-Fi with internet access, and a public STUN server helps both sides find each other. A few strict networks (some offices, some mobile carriers) block direct connections; then an admin adds a TURN relay server in Admin → Network and it works everywhere.

Can I add a claw, a laser or a fan later?

Yes. Add a switch, slider, servo or button in the Parts tab and save: the robot reloads its parts within seconds and the cockpit grows a control for it. For something new, write a short Python plugin on the robot, or let the Arduino report the part itself.

Is the robot code open?

Yes. The Python robot (aiortc for WebRTC, gpiozero for the pins) and the Arduino sketch are in the robot folder of the public GitHub repository. You can read the installer before you run it.

Who can see my camera?

Only you, the people you share the robot with, and anyone holding a guest pass you made, while they are connected. Video goes straight between browsers and the robot and is never stored by Robo Presence.

Is it free?

Yes. You pay only for your parts.
